Pay the neighbor. Clean the block.
Prove the work on a phone. Then take the same method to other cities.

If you have never heard of us, start here. Global Community Cleaners (GCC) is a Chicago charity. We pay a person who lives on a dirty city block $100 to clean that block. Every dollar of that $100 is their paycheck — we do not keep a cut. A free iPhone app (the Android version is next) lets them claim a paid block, walk both sidewalks, photograph the work, and get paid. This paper explains who we are, what already happened on 20 streets, how the phone proves it, and how a gift of any size becomes a paycheck or keeps the charity running.

What the free phone app does in a neighbor’s hand — six steps

Cards 1–5 are live iPhone screens from the GCC app. Card 6 is a real 3D model of a scanned North Lawndale block, shown in our viewer — it is not inside the worker app yet. The app is free on iPhone today. Android (the system on most phones here) is next, on a test phone UIC is sponsoring.

1Claim a funded $100 block
The home screen lists streets someone already paid for. $100.00 is the waiting paycheck. A neighbor taps the street to take the job.
2Walk both sidewalks of the block
The green shape is both sidewalks around one block. The job is the whole loop, not one pile. Photos at the corners prove they finished.
3Point the camera at the trash
A box appears around the litter. The phone stamps location (GPS — the same system car maps use) and time. The iPhone depth sensor (Apple calls it LiDAR) measures that it is a real sidewalk, not a selfie.
4$100 payout approved
Both sidewalks in. $100 approved, 100% to the worker. Money moves through Stripe, the payment company many online stores use — usually approved in 15–30 minutes, in their bank in 1–2 days.
5The neighborhood heatmap
Red means dumping that keeps coming back. A neighbor can mark a new pile, or take a smaller paid job (from $10) to clean one hotspot.
6A 3D copy of a real block
Phone depth scans build a 3D model of the actual curb, grass, and piles. That copy of real North Lawndale ground is how future cleaning robots can practice here, and how we map every place a worker logged. Not in the worker app yet.

Why we also map the pattern, not just the bag: trash returns along fences when the wind blows, and on walking streets with no public cans for blocks. The next dollar can buy a screen or a bin — not only another bag.

Why a cleaner, paid block is more than looks — the research, in plain language

  • Philadelphia vacant-lot study (Branas and colleagues, University of Pennsylvania): they randomly cleaned and greened vacant lots. Gun assaults fell 29%. People reporting depression fell 42% (69% among lower-income residents). Cities recouped about $7.43 in public value per dollar of greening.
  • Chicago neighborhood study (Robert Sampson, University of Chicago): on blocks where neighbors actually look out for one another, violent crime is about 40% lower than on similar blocks where they do not. Paying a neighbor to care for their own street is that idea with a paycheck and a photo record.
  • This neighborhood. Chicago Department of Public Health: people in the Loop live to about 87; people in West Garfield Park (next door to North Lawndale) to about 67 — a 21-year gap. Black Chicagoans died 10.6 years younger than the city average (2023). UIC Great Cities Institute: North Lawndale violent crime is about 3× the city rate. A $100 block wage will not close those gaps alone. It is the smallest honest unit of the same fight: a cleaner, paid, watched street.
