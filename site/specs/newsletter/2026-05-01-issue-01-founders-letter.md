## Sixty years and two blocks

On **January 26, 1966**, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. carried his bags up to a third-floor walk-up at **1550 South Hamlin Avenue** in North Lawndale and started what he called the "action phase" of the Chicago Freedom Movement — the campaign that brought him here to focus on housing conditions and economic opportunity in Chicago neighborhoods. He stayed about six months. He preached at Soldier Field to 30,000 in July. The campaign produced the Summit Agreement, which fed directly into the 1968 Fair Housing Act.

Today, Global Community Cleaners — the organization sending this email — is headquartered at **1844 South Harding, 2nd Floor**, a six-block walk from the apartment Dr. King moved into.

Sixty years. Two blocks of distance. Same neighborhood, same work.

This is our first newsletter. It explains why we exist, what we are doing about it, and what the next move looks like.

## What "broken windows" actually proved

Most people know the phrase. Almost no one has read the modern research.

The original 1982 essay by James Q. Wilson and George Kelling argued that visible disorder — a broken window left unfixed, trash piling up, graffiti — invites bigger problems. For four decades, the framework shaped how cities thought about neighborhoods. The intuition was right that physical conditions matter. The newer research has refined what *kind* of attention to those conditions actually moves the needle.

Three things have happened since.

**1. The theory needed an update.** A 2019 meta-analysis of roughly three hundred studies (O'Brien, Farrell & Welsh, *Social Science & Medicine*) found that the link between disorder and crime largely runs through a third factor: **collective efficacy** — the willingness of neighbors to step in for each other. Disorder and crime are both, in part, downstream of how connected the people on a block feel to one another and to the place itself.

**2. Robert Sampson's Chicago research showed what works.** Studying our city neighborhood by neighborhood, Sampson and colleagues showed that places scoring high on collective efficacy ran roughly **40% lower** crime rates — even when income, race, and disorder looked similar on paper. The lesson: invest in the people on the block, and the block follows.

**3. Then the experimental evidence rolled in.** Charles Branas and his team at Penn ran a citywide cluster randomized trial in Philadelphia — the gold standard, the kind of study that medicine uses to approve drugs. They randomized 541 vacant lots into three groups: cleaned and greened, trash-removal only, and untouched control. They watched what happened over 18 months.

> Greening the lots cut **monthly shootings by 6.8%** and **gun assaults by 29.1%** in the surrounding area.
> *— Branas et al., PNAS, February 2018*

> Residents living near greened lots reported **41.5% less feeling depressed** and **62.8% reduction in self-reported poor mental health.** For residents below the poverty line, the depression drop was **68.7%.**
> *— South, Hohl, Kondo, MacDonald & Branas, JAMA Network Open, July 2018*

The cost? About **$5 per square meter to install and 50¢ a year to maintain** (Moyer et al., *AJPH* 2019). Wharton's Analytics Center later showed that nearby home values rose 2–5% per year for houses within a quarter mile, generating roughly **$100 million a year in additional Philadelphia property tax revenue** and a **$7.43 return for every public dollar spent**.

Cleaning blocks, it turns out, is one of the highest-ROI neighborhood interventions ever measured — for safety, for mental health, and for property values. **And it works best when it's done by the people who live there.** Which brings us to what we built.

## The neighborhood we are working in

The data on North Lawndale isn't subtle.

- **Life expectancy:** A baby born in Chicago's Loop will live to **87.3**. A baby born in adjacent West Garfield Park will live to **66.6.** That's a 20-year gap inside the same city. (Chicago Department of Public Health, *2023 Life Expectancy Data Brief*, released September 2025.)
- **Citywide gap:** Black Chicagoans in 2023 were dying **10.6 years earlier than non-Black Chicagoans.** (Same source.)
- **Violent crime:** North Lawndale's per-capita violent-crime rate runs roughly **3× the citywide rate** and the community is on the city's official list of areas "most affected by violence." (UIC Great Cities Institute, *North Lawndale Service Area Databook*, 2022.)
- **Public-spending imbalance:** UIC researchers have documented hundreds of millions of public dollars flowing *out of* North Lawndale annually — through downstream costs the city absorbs in healthcare, public safety, and emergency services — versus what flows back *in* through direct neighborhood investment. Closing that gap one block at a time is what we are here to do.

This is the gap a $100 block-cleaning fee is built to close.

## Meet the founder, in his own words

The Global Community Cleaners model came from one person looking at one neighborhood and refusing to keep accepting it.

**Shaquille Tate** was born at Mt. Sinai Hospital in North Lawndale. He grew up on the blocks running from **15th Street to Ridgeway**. He drives for Divvy across the entire city for his day job, which means most days he sees what every Chicago neighborhood looks like from the curb. In a 2026 interview on the *BSV Tech Tap-In* radio show, he explained how the idea found him:

> *"I had to come back to the North Lawndale community, and I'm walking past abandoned houses — boards on the windows, broken windows, trash everywhere on the floor."*

He had been reading about the broken-windows theory. He knew the academic framing. But he was looking for a different lever:

> *"What if somebody could get all these kids to clean up their block? And if they were to clean up their block, to get paid to clean up their block."*

Then the kicker — the line that became the entire model:

> *"I'm employing elders and I'm employing youth and I'm paying them a hundred dollars to clean up their blocks. I want to start with a 10-block radius… This is more than just picking up trash."*

He's right. It's about cleaning, but it's also about cash, dignity, ownership, and the simple fact that **the people best equipped to take care of a block are the people who live on it** — if someone is willing to pay them like the work matters. Because it does.

## How GCC actually works

The model is plain on purpose.

| Element | What it means |
|---|---|
| **$100 / block** | The full price to a sponsor for one cleanup. **100% of it goes to the resident** doing the work. GCC's overhead is funded separately. |
| **One participant per block** | The person who lives there cleans their own street, gate to crest, both sides, including the alley. They know it best. They have the standing to maintain it. |
| **Elders and youth** | Both. The model is intergenerational by design. |
| **Same-day pay** | No 30-day waiting. You finish the work, you get paid. |
| **10-block radius to start** | The pilot is concentrated in a tight cluster around the 1844 S. Harding office so we can prove the model and scale it. |
| **Before-and-after photos** | Sponsors receive proof. Block status is logged in our system. The same measurement discipline that turned greening research from a hunch into evidence-based, peer-reviewed practice. |

A $100 block sponsorship gets your name on our public sponsor wall. A **$2,000+ sponsorship** unlocks the recognition tier — name on the wall, digital certificate of appreciation, 501(c)(3) tax receipt, monthly newsletter feature, and social-media recognition across our channels.
