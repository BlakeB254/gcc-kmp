The Problem & Our Solution

Chicago has seventy-seven community areas. Many on the South and West sides struggle with illegal dumping, accumulated trash, and aging infrastructure. The city's 311 system captures complaints but not resolutions—you can report a dirty alley, but there is no verification that anyone cleaned it, no before-and-after documentation, and no data on what was actually there. The gap between reporting and resolution is where accountability disappears.

GCC changes this. When a community member spots trash on their block, they open our app, photograph the scene, and in under ten seconds, the system does something remarkable: it identifies twelve distinct categories of waste in the image, estimates the volume of the pile, scores its severity on a five-point scale, and pins it to a precise GPS coordinate. Their report is live.

Within twenty-four hours, another community member walking the same stretch of road receives a notification: there is an unverified spot nearby. They walk over, confirm the mess is still there, and tap "verify." The system checks that the verifier is within two hundred meters of the original report, that they are not the same person who submitted it, and that their device is capturing a live photo—not one pulled from a camera roll. The spot is now verified. It is no longer one person's complaint. It is a corroborated, GPS-locked, AI-classified data point.

A community worker—someone who has signed up through GCC and received basic training—claims the block. They walk it with GPS tracking active, stopping at every documented spot. They clean each one, take an after photo at the same coordinates, and the system automatically creates a before-and-after pair that proves the work was done. The entire lifecycle, from a neighbor's frustration to a clean corner with photographic evidence, is captured in structured data.

Every person in that chain earns something. The resident who reported the spot earns ten points. The neighbor who verified it earns five. The worker who cleaned it earns fifty, plus pay from the sponsor who funded that block. Because behind every block in GCC, there is a sponsor—a local business, a church, a foundation, a concerned neighbor across town—who has put up money to say, this stretch of sidewalk matters to me. It is a marketplace where community care meets capital, and every transaction is documented.

This is what GCC does. We are not a cleanup crew with an app. We are a system that turns the invisible labor of neighborhood maintenance into visible, verified, rewarded civic participation. We started in North Lawndale because it is one of Chicago's most underserved communities, and because the people who live there deserve the same quality of environmental data that wealthier neighborhoods take for granted. But the model is designed for all seventy-seven of Chicago's community areas—and eventually, for any city willing to let its residents lead.

The Data

The real innovation of GCC is not the cleanup. It is the data that the cleanup produces.

Every spot report that enters our system carries more than twenty-five structured fields: GPS coordinates, timestamps, AI-generated waste classification across twelve categories, severity scores, estimated volume and weight, device attestation metadata, before-and-after photo pairs, and resolution status. On iPhone Pro devices, we use LiDAR depth scanning to generate precise volumetric measurements of waste piles—turning a photograph into a three-dimensional record. Each report is linked to its community area, ward, police district, and census tract, so the data can be cross-referenced with any other civic dataset in Chicago.

This data is formatted to be SODA-compatible—the same standard used by the Chicago Data Portal. That is deliberate. GCC is pursuing integration with the City of Chicago's Data Portal to contribute verified community cleanliness data directly to the city's open data infrastructure. Right now, the 311 system captures complaints, but it does not capture resolutions. A resident can call in an illegal dump, but there is no structured record of whether it was cleaned, when, by whom, or what the site looked like before and after. GCC provides that complete lifecycle: report, verify, clean, document. It is the missing half of the city's environmental data.

We have built a cleanliness scoring model that produces a composite score for any block, route, or community area. The score weighs the number of active spots, the average time to resolution, the recurrence rate of waste at the same coordinates, and the severity distribution of reports. Over time, this creates a longitudinal picture of environmental quality that no survey or satellite image can match, because it is verified at street level by the people who live there.

The anti-fraud architecture matters, because without it the data is not trustworthy. Every submission requires a live-capture photo—no uploads from camera rolls. GPS coordinates are locked at capture time and cross-referenced with device sensors. Verifiers must be within two hundred meters and cannot verify their own reports. A fifty-meter deduplication radius prevents the same spot from being reported twice. Device attestation records the phone model, operating system, and capture method for every single data point. This is not self-reported survey data. It is instrument-grade civic measurement.

This is the kind of structured, verified environmental data that does not exist anywhere else in civic technology. It is granular enough for block-level analysis, standardized enough for citywide comparison, and longitudinal enough to measure change over time. And it is generated not by municipal employees or academic field teams, but by the communities themselves.

Community Incentives

The hardest problem in civic technology is not building the tool. It is getting people to use it consistently, week after week, when the novelty wears off and the work is still there. GCC's answer is a gamification system designed not as a gimmick, but as a behavioral engine rooted in the same incentive principles that behavioral economists have studied for decades.

The system recognizes five distinct actions, each worth a different number of points. Submitting a spot report earns ten points. Verifying a report—confirming that a mess is still there—earns five points, while verifying that a spot has already been cleaned earns ten, because it closes the loop and generates valuable resolution data. Volunteering to clean a spot earns fifty points. Reporting that a location is already clean earns five. These values were not chosen arbitrarily. They reflect the relative effort, data value, and community impact of each action, and they create a feedback loop where every form of participation is recognized.

On top of the point system, we have built a badge framework with fifty-four badges spread across nine categories: Jobs, Observation, Volunteer, First Response, Streaks, Community, Earnings, Early Adopter, and Sponsorship. Each category has up to five progression tiers—Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum, and Diamond—so that a resident who starts by casually reporting a dirty alley has a visible path toward becoming one of their neighborhood's most recognized community stewards. Every badge earned is displayed on the user's public profile, and community leaderboards make sustained participation visible to neighbors and sponsors alike.

Points translate into tangible rewards through a prize pool that we are building with local partners. The vision is straightforward: Chicago's sports teams, museums, and cultural attractions have unsold inventory—tickets to a Tuesday night Bulls game, weekday passes to the Shedd Aquarium, family four-packs at Six Flags. Donating that inventory to GCC's prize pool is a tax-deductible contribution for the donor and a powerful motivator for community members who might not otherwise see a reason to pick up a broom for their block. A family outing to a Cubs game because their mom spent Saturday mornings keeping the block clean—that is the connection between community service and real experience that we are building.

We are also developing a bounty system where sponsors can fund the cleanup of specific spots, creating a direct marketplace where someone who wants to see a particular corner cleaned is connected to the person willing to do the work. It is behavioral economics in action: variable rewards, visible progress, social recognition, and tangible outcomes, all layered on top of genuine civic participation. Every point earned, every badge unlocked, every bounty claimed generates data about how incentive structures affect sustained community engagement—data that is testable, publishable, and applicable far beyond Chicago.

Partnership Ecosystem

GCC is pursuing partnerships at three levels, and we want to be transparent about where we are and what we need—because some of these conversations are ones where a well-connected partner can make a meaningful difference.

City & Government

Our primary target is a formal data partnership with the Chicago Data Portal. GCC's SODA-compatible export format was built specifically for this integration. The goal is for GCC to become an official data provider for the city's environmental monitoring infrastructure—filling the gap between 311 complaint intake and on-the-ground resolution tracking. Beyond data integration, there are opportunities in environmental consulting, analytics contracts, and helping the city measure the effectiveness of its own cleanup operations with verified, structured evidence.

Local Partners — Prize Donors

Chicago's professional sports franchises—the Bulls, Bears, Cubs, White Sox, Blackhawks, Fire FC, and Sky—along with major cultural institutions like the Field Museum, Shedd Aquarium, Museum of Science and Industry, and the Art Institute of Chicago, plus attractions like Six Flags and Navy Pier, and transit systems like CTA and Divvy. The ask is simple: donate unsold tickets and passes to GCC's community reward prize pool. For them, it is a tax-deductible contribution of inventory that would otherwise go unused. For our community members, it is the difference between an abstract point total and a real experience—a family outing earned through service. Every donated ticket tells a community that these institutions care about where they live.

National Technology Partners

GCC's technology stack creates natural alignment with major technology companies. NVIDIA's edge computing and AI inference platforms are directly relevant to our on-device waste classification models. Meta's smart glasses represent a hands-free documentation tool that could transform how workers capture data in the field. Apple's LiDAR scanning technology is already integrated into our iPhone Pro workflow for volumetric waste measurement. Google's mapping infrastructure and nonprofit cloud credits program could underwrite the geospatial backbone of our entire system. These are not speculative partnerships—they are engineering fits, and each company has existing nonprofit and civic technology programs through which GCC would be a compelling candidate.

If your organization has connections to any of these partners, an introduction would be enormously valuable. We are building something new—and we need allies at every level.

Research & Collaboration

GCC's dataset and deployment model create concrete research opportunities across multiple disciplines. We want to lay them out plainly so potential partners can see where GCC fits into work they may already be doing.

Urban Studies — Block-level cleanliness data offers a new proxy for neighborhood investment and equity. Cleanliness correlates with property values, health outcomes, public safety perceptions, and commercial activity. Our data makes it possible to measure these correlations at a granularity that has not been available before: not by zip code, but by individual city block, tracked over time, with photographic evidence.

Computer Science — Our on-device AI models classify waste into twelve categories and estimate severity, but they are still early. There are open problems in model accuracy, training data expansion with labeled Chicago-specific imagery, LiDAR-based volume estimation, and optimal cleanup routing—given a set of verified spots, what is the most efficient path for a worker to walk?

Public Policy — Can gamified civic participation be sustained over time, and does it produce measurable improvements in neighborhood conditions? GCC is generating the longitudinal data to answer that question, but it needs rigorous study design, confounding controls, and published findings that other cities can act on.

Behavioral Economics — Our points-and-badges system is a real-world incentive experiment running continuously in underserved communities. Do monetary bounties crowd out intrinsic motivation or complement it? Does the badge progression matter more than the point total? Does a public leaderboard increase participation in competitive individuals while discouraging others? The data exists. It needs rigorous analysis.

Data Science & Environmental Science — Predictive modeling for waste accumulation, early-warning systems for illegal dumping hotspots, and composition analysis across all seventy-seven community areas—revealing patterns in how waste varies by neighborhood demographics, commercial activity, and proximity to transit.

What we need from partners is not complicated. We need researchers who want to work with a novel civic dataset. We need the credibility signal that comes from institutional partnership. And we need access to the policy, philanthropic, and civic networks where this kind of work gets noticed and funded. In return, partners get something that is genuinely hard to find: a real-world civic technology deployment generating structured, verified data in Chicago's own neighborhoods—a research platform, a case study, and a publication pipeline, all in one.
