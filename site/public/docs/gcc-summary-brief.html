An Overview for the University of Chicago

How Community Technology Is Transforming Neighborhood Cleanliness in Chicago

March 2026

globalcommunitycleaners.org

What GCC Does

Imagine a resident in North Lawndale walking to work on a Monday morning. She passes a vacant lot on Roosevelt Road where someone has dumped a mattress, a dozen trash bags, and a pile of construction debris. She has seen this corner deteriorate for months. Calling 311 feels futile—she has done it before, and nothing changed. But today she opens the GCC app on her phone, takes a photo, and in under ten seconds, the system does something remarkable: it identifies twelve distinct categories of waste in the image, estimates the volume of the pile, scores its severity on a five-point scale, and pins it to a precise GPS coordinate. Her report is live.

Within twenty-four hours, another community member walking the same stretch of Roosevelt Road receives a notification: there is an unverified spot nearby. He walks over, confirms the mess is still there, and taps "verify." The system checks that he is within two hundred meters of the original report, that he is not the same person who submitted it, and that his device is capturing a live photo—not one pulled from a camera roll. The spot is now verified. It is no longer one person's complaint. It is a corroborated, GPS-locked, AI-classified data point.

A community worker—someone who has signed up through GCC, passed a background check, and received basic training—claims the block. She walks it with GPS tracking active, stopping at every documented spot. She cleans each one, takes an after photo at the same coordinates, and the system automatically creates a before-and-after pair that proves the work was done. The entire lifecycle, from a neighbor's frustration to a clean corner with photographic evidence, is captured in structured data.

Every person in that chain earned something. The woman who reported the spot earned ten points. The man who verified it earned five. The worker who cleaned it earned fifty, plus pay from the sponsor who funded that block. Because behind every block in GCC, there is a sponsor—a local business, a church, a concerned resident across town—who has put up a hundred dollars to say, this stretch of sidewalk matters to me. It is a marketplace where care meets capital, and every transaction is documented.

This is what GCC does. We are not a cleanup crew with an app. We are a system that turns the invisible labor of neighborhood maintenance into visible, verified, rewarded civic participation. We started in North Lawndale because it is one of Chicago's most underserved communities, and because the people who live there deserve the same quality of environmental data that wealthier neighborhoods take for granted. But the model is designed for all seventy-seven of Chicago's community areas, and eventually, for any city willing to let its residents lead.

The Data Story

The real innovation of GCC is not the cleanup. It is the data that the cleanup produces.

Every spot report that enters our system carries more than twenty-five structured fields: GPS coordinates, timestamps, AI-generated waste classification across twelve categories, severity scores, estimated volume and weight, device attestation metadata, before-and-after photo pairs, and resolution status. On iPhone Pro devices, we use LiDAR depth scanning to generate precise volumetric measurements of waste piles—turning a photograph into a three-dimensional record. Each report is linked to its community area, ward, police district, and census tract, so the data can be cross-referenced with any other civic dataset in Chicago.

This data is formatted to be SODA-compatible—the same standard used by the Chicago Data Portal. That is deliberate. We are actively pursuing a data partnership with the City of Chicago to contribute verified community cleanliness data directly to the Data Portal. Right now, the city's 311 system captures complaints, but it does not capture resolutions. A resident can call in an illegal dump, but there is no structured record of whether it was cleaned, when, by whom, or what the site looked like before and after. GCC provides that complete lifecycle: report, verify, clean, document. It is the missing half of the city's environmental data.

We have built a cleanliness scoring model that produces a composite score for any block, route, or community area. The score weighs the number of active spots, the average time to resolution, the recurrence rate of waste at the same coordinates, and the severity distribution of reports. Over time, this creates a longitudinal picture of environmental quality that no survey or satellite image can match, because it is verified at street level by the people who live there.

The anti-fraud architecture matters here, because without it the data is not trustworthy. Every submission requires a live-capture photo—no uploads from camera rolls. GPS coordinates are locked at capture time and cross-referenced with device sensors. Verifiers must be within two hundred meters and cannot verify their own reports. A fifty-meter deduplication radius prevents the same spot from being reported twice. Device attestation records the phone model, operating system, and capture method for every single data point. This is not self-reported survey data. It is instrument-grade civic measurement.

For researchers, the implication is straightforward: this is the kind of structured, verified environmental data that does not exist anywhere else. It is granular enough for block-level analysis, standardized enough for citywide comparison, and longitudinal enough to measure change over time. And it is generated not by municipal employees or academic field teams, but by the communities themselves.

The Gamification Model

The hardest problem in civic technology is not building the tool. It is getting people to use it consistently, week after week, when the novelty wears off and the work is still there. GCC's answer to this is a gamification system designed not as a gimmick, but as a behavioral engine rooted in the same incentive principles that behavioral economists at institutions like UChicago have studied for decades.

The system recognizes five distinct actions, each worth a different number of points. Submitting a spot report earns ten points. Verifying a report—confirming that a mess is still there—earns five points, while verifying that a spot has already been cleaned earns ten (because it closes the loop and generates valuable resolution data). Volunteering to clean a spot earns fifty points. Reporting that a location is already clean earns five. These values were not chosen arbitrarily. They reflect the relative effort, data value, and community impact of each action, and they create a feedback loop where every form of participation is recognized.

On top of the point system, we have built a badge framework with fifty-four badges spread across nine categories: Jobs, Observation, Volunteer, First Response, Streaks, Community, Earnings, Early Adopter, and Sponsorship. Each badge category has up to five progression tiers—Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum, and Diamond—so that a resident who starts by casually reporting a dirty alley has a visible path toward becoming one of her neighborhood's most recognized community stewards. The progression is not abstract. Every badge earned is displayed on the user's public profile, and community leaderboards make sustained participation visible to neighbors and sponsors alike.

Points translate into tangible rewards through a prize pool that we are building with local partners. The vision is straightforward: Chicago's sports teams, museums, and attractions have unsold inventory—tickets to a Tuesday night Bulls game, weekday passes to the Shedd Aquarium, family four-packs at Six Flags. Donating that inventory to GCC's prize pool is a tax-deductible contribution for them and a powerful motivator for community members who might not otherwise see a reason to pick up a broom for their block. We are also developing a bounty system where sponsors can fund the cleanup of specific spots, creating a direct connection between someone who wants to see a particular corner cleaned and the person willing to do the work.

For researchers in behavioral economics and public policy, this system is a living laboratory. Every point earned, every badge unlocked, every bounty claimed generates data about how incentive structures affect sustained civic participation. Does the badge progression matter more than the point total? Do bounties crowd out volunteer cleanups or complement them? Does the presence of a leaderboard increase participation in competitive individuals while discouraging others? These are testable hypotheses, and GCC generates the data to test them at scale, in real communities, with real stakes.

Partnerships We're Building

GCC is pursuing partnerships at three levels, and we want to be transparent about where we are and what we need—because some of these conversations are ones where the University of Chicago's network could make a meaningful difference.

At the city level, our primary target is a formal data partnership with the Chicago Data Portal. GCC's SODA-compatible export format was built specifically for this integration. The goal is for GCC to become an official data provider for the city's environmental monitoring infrastructure—filling the gap between 311 complaint intake and on-the-ground resolution tracking. We have begun outreach to the Department of Innovation and Technology, but a warm introduction from a trusted institutional partner would accelerate the conversation considerably.

At the local level, we are approaching Chicago's professional sports franchises—the Bulls, Bears, Cubs, White Sox, Blackhawks, Fire, and Sky—along with major cultural institutions like the Field Museum, Shedd Aquarium, and Museum of Science and Industry, and attractions like Six Flags and Navy Pier. The ask is simple: donate unsold tickets and passes to GCC's community reward prize pool. For them, it is a tax-deductible contribution of inventory that would otherwise go unused. For our community members, it is the difference between an abstract point total and a real experience—a family outing to a Cubs game because their mom spent Saturday mornings keeping their block clean. Every donated ticket tells a community that these institutions care about where they live.

At the national level, GCC's technology stack creates natural alignment with major technology companies. NVIDIA's edge computing and AI inference platforms are directly relevant to our on-device waste classification models. Meta's smart glasses represent a hands-free documentation tool that could transform how workers capture data in the field. Apple's LiDAR scanning technology is already integrated into our iPhone Pro workflow for volumetric waste measurement. Google's mapping infrastructure and nonprofit cloud credits program could underwrite the geospatial backbone of our entire system. These are not speculative partnerships—they are engineering fits, and each company has existing nonprofit and civic technology programs through which GCC would be a compelling candidate.

The ask to UChicago is specific: if you have existing relationships with any of these organizations—through alumni networks, corporate partnerships, research collaborations, or board connections—an introduction or a forwarded pitch would be enormously valuable. We are not asking the University to fundraise on our behalf. We are asking you to open doors where your network overlaps with our needs. A single email from a trusted UChicago contact to the right person at the Bulls Foundation or NVIDIA's AI for Good program could be the difference between a cold outreach and a real conversation.

Research Collaboration

We see concrete research opportunities across at least six departments at the University of Chicago, and we want to lay them out plainly so you can see where GCC fits into work you may already be doing.

In Urban Studies, GCC's block-level cleanliness data offers a new proxy for neighborhood investment and equity. Cleanliness is not just an aesthetic issue—it correlates with property values, health outcomes, public safety perceptions, and commercial activity. Our data makes it possible to measure these correlations at a granularity that has not been available before: not by zip code or community area, but by individual city block, tracked over time, with photographic evidence.

In Computer Science, the on-device AI models that classify waste into twelve categories and estimate severity are still early. We would welcome collaboration on improving model accuracy, expanding the training dataset with labeled Chicago-specific imagery, and refining our LiDAR-based volume estimation pipeline. There is also interesting work to be done on optimal cleanup routing—given a set of verified spots, what is the most efficient path for a worker to walk?

In Public Policy, the central question is whether gamified civic participation can be sustained over time, and whether it produces measurable improvements in neighborhood conditions. GCC is generating the longitudinal data to answer that question, but we need policy researchers to design the studies, control for confounders, and publish findings that can inform other cities considering similar models.

In Behavioral Economics, our points-and-badges system is a real-world incentive experiment running continuously in underserved communities. How do different reward structures affect participation rates? Do monetary bounties crowd out intrinsic motivation or complement it? Does public recognition through leaderboards change behavior differently in tight-knit neighborhoods versus more transient ones? The data exists. It needs rigorous analysis.

In Data Science, there are open questions about predictive modeling: can we forecast waste accumulation based on historical patterns, weather, events, and land use? Can we build early-warning models for illegal dumping hotspots? And in Environmental Science, GCC's twelve-category waste classification system creates the foundation for composition analysis across all seventy-seven community areas—a dataset that could reveal patterns in how waste varies by neighborhood demographics, commercial activity, and proximity to transit.

What we need from UChicago is not complicated. We need student researchers who want to work with a novel civic dataset. We need the credibility signal that comes from an academic partnership with one of the world's great research universities. And we need access to the urban planning and policy networks where this kind of work gets noticed and funded. In return, UChicago gets something that is genuinely hard to find: a real-world civic technology deployment generating structured, verified data in Chicago's own neighborhoods—a research platform, a case study, and a publication pipeline, all in one.

"We believe GCC represents a new model for community-driven environmental data collection—one that merges technology, incentives, and verifiable impact. We would welcome the opportunity to explore how the University of Chicago can help shape what this becomes."
